The Pan Heat Guide

Most pan cooking problems are heat problems. Learn to read the pan and you will stop burning some things and steaming others.

Why heat matters

Browning, the flavour reaction that makes seared food taste of something, only happens fast above about 140°C. Below that, food sweats and steams. Far above what the food needs, the outside burns before the inside cooks. Good pan cooking is simply putting each food in the right band and keeping it there.

Reading heat without a thermometer

  • Water drop test. Flick a few drops on the dry pan. Sizzle and evaporate in a second or two: medium. Bead up and dance: hot, roughly 200°C.
  • Hand test. Hold your palm about 5 cm above the surface. Five seconds before you must pull away is medium, three is medium-high, one is high.
  • Oil shimmer. Oil that ripples and flows like water is ready for most cooking. Oil that smokes is too hot.

An infrared thermometer is inexpensive and takes the guesswork out entirely if you cook on a griddle often.

Why food sticks

Protein bonds to metal when it first touches the pan. As a crust forms, the bond breaks and the food releases. Sticking almost always has one of three causes:

  1. The pan was not hot enough when the food went in.
  2. The food was wet, so it steamed instead of searing.
  3. It was moved before the crust had formed.

Give it time. If it resists a gentle nudge with a spatula, it is not ready.

Choosing the fat

Use oils with a high smoke point and neutral flavour for searing: sunflower, rapeseed, groundnut or refined avocado oil. Butter burns at around 150°C, so add it at the end for flavour, or use ghee, which is clarified and handles much more heat. Use only a thin film; excess oil smokes and spits.

Managing a crowded pan

Every piece of cold food you add lowers the temperature. Put too much in at once and the pan drops below browning heat. Cook in batches, keep finished food warm in a low oven, and let the pan recover for a minute between batches.

Carryover heat

Thick pieces of meat keep cooking for a few minutes after they leave the pan as heat moves inward. Take them off slightly before they reach the doneness you want, rest them on a warm plate, and they will finish on their own.
